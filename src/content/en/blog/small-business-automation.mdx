Copying a quote request into a spreadsheet. Chasing a customer who never replied. Reminding someone of tomorrow's appointment. Each task takes a few minutes, but added up, they eat your evenings. **Small business automation means handing off to software whatever repeats the same way every time**, so you keep your time for the work that pays.

**Short answer:** start with one task that's frequent, repetitive and easy to describe ("when a lead comes in, send a confirmation and add it to the sheet"). Automate it, test it on real cases for two weeks, measure the time saved, then move on to the next one. Keep anything that requires judgment in human hands.

<Encadre>

## 8 small business automation ideas

Here are the most common automations in a small business, from the simplest to the most complete. *Setup times are rough, indicative ranges.*

| Task | What the automation does | Setup |
|---|---|---|
| Reply to a new lead | Instant confirmation with your callback time | A few hours |
| Appointment reminder | Text or email the day before, with address and time | A few hours |
| Review request | Message with your Google profile link after the job | A few hours |
| Quote follow-up | Spaced follow-ups until the quote is signed or declined | One day |
| Centralize leads | Form, email and calls in one sheet or CRM | One day |
| Missed call | Automatic "we'll call you back" text when no one picks up | One day |
| Weekly report | Leads, quotes and wins sent every Monday | One to two days |
| Qualify leads | Automatic questions (area, budget, timing) before you call | A few days |

<Encadre type="exemple" titre="Illustrative example: quote follow-up">
A remodeling company sends quotes by email. Before: follow-ups depended on whether there was time on Friday. After: three days after sending, the customer gets a short message ("Did you get a chance to look at our proposal? Happy to talk it through"), then a second one a week later. As soon as the quote is marked signed or declined in the sheet, follow-ups stop.
</Encadre>

<Encadre type="attention" titre="Pitfalls to avoid">
- **Automating everything at once.** A mistake in a complex workflow is hard to find. One task at a time.
- **Forgetting to check.** A broken automation sends the wrong message to all your customers. Test, then check in regularly.
- **Robotic messages.** Write automated messages the way you'd talk to a customer, with your name and a real phone number.
- **Ignoring consent.** Marketing texts need prior consent in the US. Keep automated outreach to people who opted in.
</Encadre>

## Measuring the time you save

An automation is judged on facts. Before you launch it, note how much time the task took each week. After a month, compare: time spent, leads handled, reply time, quotes signed. To track these numbers effortlessly, you need to know where your leads come from: that's what our [conversion tracking agency](/en/services/data-tracking) work is about.

And if leads are short upstream, start at the source: a website that brings in contacts (see [how to make a small business website](/en/blog/how-to-make-a-small-business-website)) and a local presence that helps you [get construction leads](/en/blog/construction-leads) without depending on lead platforms.

## Where to start

Small business automation isn't about finding the perfect tool: it's about finding the task that costs you the most time or customers. Take the quiz at the top of this article, pick one task, automate it, test it for two weeks and measure. For most small businesses, lead replies and quote follow-ups are the two best places to start.
